Stars usually change over millions of years. This one changed in about seven.

## What happened

Astronomers say they've reconstructed, for the first time, what happened before, during and after a smaller star spiraled into a giant one and merged with it, in our neighboring **Andromeda galaxy**. The research, led by **Viraj Karambelkar**, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University, was published in **Nature** on **Wednesday, November 18, 2026**, according to [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasa-data-clinches-first-of-its-kind-stellar-merger/). [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/stars-andromeda-galaxy-merge-little-bao) also covered the find.

The team nicknamed the system **Bao** (formally **AT 2025abao**).

## The two stars

Based on the team's modeling, NASA says:

- **"Big Bao"** was about **7 times the Sun's mass, 350 times its radius and 18,000 times brighter**, a puffy supergiant.
- **"Little Bao"** was about **0.7 times the Sun's mass**, slightly smaller and fainter than our Sun.

Once the small star touched the giant's outer layers, it took only about **seven orbits, roughly seven years**, to merge. Other known mergers involving two smaller stars took thousands of orbits. The analysis found Little Bao was dragged into Big Bao's core. Co-author Kishalay De described it as "almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star."

## How they caught it

Mergers are hard to predict, so this one took a lot of telescopes and some luck, per NASA's timeline:

- **Feb. 2023:** NASA's retired NEOWISE mission flags an unusual variable star.
- **Sept. 2024:** the WINTER telescope sees it brightening and shedding dust.
- **July 2025:** NASA's SPHEREx happens to catch it fading in visible light and brightening in infrared.
- **Oct. 17, 2025:** the Zwicky Transient Facility detects the eruption.
- **Dec. 24, 2025:** the **James Webb Space Telescope** observes it just **47 days** after the eruption peaked, the earliest such follow-up ever.

Webb's mid-infrared data on the dust the giant star had shed over centuries let the team pick the simulation that matched and pin down the masses.

## Why it matters

When two stars share a common envelope of gas, sometimes they merge and sometimes both survive in a tight orbit. Survivors can later become neutron stars, black holes, sources of gravitational waves or millisecond pulsars. Watching one play out in real time lets astronomers measure how that process works instead of reverse-engineering it from ancient events. Karambelkar says NASA's new Roman Space Telescope should turn up many more.
