A launch pad usually needs days of cleanup and checks between rockets. SpaceX's West Coast crew just did it in about a day and a third.

## What happened

Two Falcon 9 rockets lifted off from the same pad, **Space Launch Complex 4E at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California**, less than a day and a half apart:

- **USSF-317** launched Friday, **November 20 at 8:20 PM PT** for the U.S. Space Force, per [SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com/launches/ussf317). Its booster landed on the droneship *Of Course I Still Love You* in the Pacific.
- **NROL-136** launched Sunday, **November 22 at 4:23 AM PT** for the National Reconnaissance Office, per [SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com/launches/nrol136) and the [NRO](https://www.nro.gov/news-media-featured-stories/news-media-press-releases/Archived-Press-Releases/Press-Release-Article/Article/4655832/nro-successfully-launches-nrol-136-november-22/). That booster came back to land at Landing Zone 4 near the pad.

"32 hr, 3 min, 21 sec! This smashed the previous record of 45 hrs from Pad 40," SpaceX vice president of launch Kiko Dontchev posted on X, as quoted by [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-spy-satellite-launch-nrol-136-proliferated-architecture). He added the team had "several hours of margin" and set a new goal: "the 24 hr turn!" Pad 40 is SpaceX's busy pad at Cape Canaveral in Florida.

## What went up

Both payloads are classified.

- **USSF-317** carried satellites for the Space Force's Space Data Network Backbone, a secure military communications network being built under a SpaceX contract, according to SpaceNews.
- **NROL-136** was the NRO's **15th launch** for its "proliferated architecture," a large network of smaller spy satellites built with SpaceX and Northrop Grumman to provide more frequent coverage of places around the globe. The NRO calls it "the largest satellite constellation in U.S. government history." It has not said how many satellites were aboard.

The NROL-136 booster was flying for the **14th time**; it had previously launched NASA's Sentinel-6B ocean satellite and eight Starlink missions, among others.

## Why it matters

Pad turnaround time is one of the biggest limits on how often anyone can launch. Faster turns mean more missions per year from the same concrete, which matters for the government's push to put up large constellations quickly and for SpaceX's own Starlink network. Vandenberg's range says it has already supported 77 launches this year as of USSF-317. A 24-hour turn, if SpaceX gets there, would let a single pad support a launch nearly every day.

Residents of Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo and Ventura counties may have heard sonic booms during both launches, SpaceX said.
