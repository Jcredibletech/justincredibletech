Four teeth from two caves in southern China suggest that more than one kind of archaic human lived there around **100,000 years ago**. In a study published **Wednesday, Nov. 25** in [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11341-6), researchers say two teeth belong to a **Denisovan**-related population, while a tooth from the second cave doesn't match any established human group. The authors call it an "unresolved archaic *Homo*."

## What happened

The teeth came from the **Longchuandong** and **Chuanshandong** caves in the Guangxi region. Uranium-series dating puts the Chuanshandong teeth at **at least 100,800 years old** and the Longchuandong teeth between about **104,500 and 42,600 years old**.

The team, led by researchers at China's Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology, studied the teeth's shape and pulled ancient **proteins** out of the enamel. That matters because DNA rarely survives in warm climates like southern China, but tooth enamel proteins can.

- **Longchuandong:** the tooth shapes and protein variants closely match known Denisovans, the extinct cousins of Neanderthals first identified from a finger bone in Siberia in 2010.
- **Chuanshandong:** the teeth show a mix of old and newer traits, including a complex root system like *Homo erectus*. The proteins carry an ancestral variant shared with *Homo antecessor* and great apes, plus a variant found in East Asian *H. erectus*, but lack a marker linked to Denisovans.

As described by [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/human-evolution/scientists-may-have-discovered-new-branch-on-the-human-family-tree-100-000-year-old-tooth-in-china-suggests), the protein analysis placed Chuanshandong as a sister group to the common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans.

## Not a new species yet

The researchers are careful here. "We could call it the Chuanshandong group temporarily, but we need more fossil and molecular evidence to determine its affinity with other archaic Homo," study co-author **Qiaomei Fu** told Live Science. "It's not clear whether they are our cousins."

University of Wisconsin-Madison anthropologist **John Hawks**, who wasn't involved, told Live Science he wouldn't call it "unknown *Homo*." He noted it's still unclear whether *H. erectus* or Denisovans carried that protein variant, so the teeth could turn out to be from a late *H. erectus*. No Denisovan bicuspids, the type of tooth found at Chuanshandong, have been found yet for comparison.

## Why it matters

East Asia's human story has been getting more crowded. Scientists have increasingly tied many Chinese fossils to Denisovans. This study suggests at least one other lineage may have lived alongside them in the same subtropical region. It fits with other recent finds we've covered, like [teeth showing *Homo erectus* lived in rainforest in China](/blog/homo-erectus-rainforest-china-teeth/).

## What's next

Fu says the team has more new finds from the region under analysis. Hawks says combining all the teeth in one analysis would give a stronger answer. For now, it's four teeth and a big open question.
