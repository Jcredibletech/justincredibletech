Most donor organs last only hours on ice. A team in Sydney says it kept a human liver working for nearly two months.

## What happened

Researchers led by **Carlo Pulitano**, a transplant surgeon at **Royal Prince Alfred Hospital** and a professor at the University of Sydney, say they kept a donated human liver alive outside the body for about **seven weeks**, [MIT Technology Review reported](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/19/1148934/a-human-liver-has-been-kept-alive-outside-the-body-for-seven-weeks-setting-a-record/) on November 19, 2026. The team's [conference abstract](https://journals.lww.com/transplantjournal/fulltext/10.1097/01.tp.0001251212.73596.b5~3813-towards-next-generation-organ-preservation---molecular) in the journal *Transplantation* describes livers perfused for up to 47 days.

The liver was hooked to the **Extended Organ Perfusion System**, automated pumps and sensors that supply blood and nutrients and clear waste at body temperature (about 37°C). Pulitano compared it to "an artificial body." The previous record for an internal organ outside the body was about **two weeks**, set by a Swiss team.

The abstract says eight livers were perfused on the system, all recovered function within 24 to 48 hours (judged by measures like bile production, glucose handling and lactate clearance), and the longest lasted 47 days. Tissue studies showed the liver structure was preserved, though cells formed unusually large mitochondria. Pulitano said automation was the key: on the automated system, average survival tripled to about four weeks.

## What stage this is at

This is **early research, not a treatment**. The livers were not transplanted into anyone, and the full results are **still awaiting formal publication**. Waldemar Hoffmann, CEO of the perfusion startup Apersys, told Technology Review that people in the field believe the record is real, but it isn't yet clear how healthy the organ was at the end. "The key question is what the purpose for a seven-week perfusion would be," he said.

The next step is smaller: Pulitano's startup, Aeternum Life Technologies, will help with a planned Sydney trial transplanting lower-quality livers kept on perfusion for **four days**.

## Why it matters

Organs that can wait longer could be shared over longer distances and checked more carefully before surgery, which could mean fewer wasted organs. Researchers also see long-term perfusion as a way to test drugs on living human organs, and Pulitano has speculated about one day regrowing split livers on the machine. Those ideas are speculative for now.
