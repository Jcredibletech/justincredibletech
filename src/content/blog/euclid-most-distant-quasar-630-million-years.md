How did a black hole get 360 million times heavier than the Sun when the universe was only about 5% of its current age? That's the puzzle behind a new record from Europe's Euclid space telescope.

## What happened

The European Space Agency [announced Tuesday](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/science/euclid/euclid-breaks-own-quasar-record-by-40-million-years) that Euclid has found the **most distant quasar ever seen**. A quasar is the extremely bright core of a galaxy, lit up by gas falling into a supermassive black hole.

- **Name:** J1504+6252, nicknamed **Dún Aonghasa** after a prehistoric cliff-top fort on Ireland's Atlantic coast.
- **Distance:** a redshift of **8.13**, making it the first quasar ever found beyond redshift 8. We're seeing it as it was about **630 million years after the Big Bang**, roughly 13.1 billion years ago.
- **Black hole:** about **360 million times the Sun's mass**, measured with the James Webb Space Telescope.
- **Record:** it beats Euclid's own previous record, announced a few months ago, by about 40 million years.

Euclid spotted it as a faint red dot among millions of objects in about 18 months of survey data. The W.M. Keck Observatory, the Large Binocular Telescope, Hubble and finally Webb confirmed what it was, according to the [Max Planck Institute for Astronomy](https://www.mpia.de/news/science/2026-10-dun-aonghasa), whose former postdoc Julien Wolf led the team. The paper is published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*.

## Why it matters

The closer to the Big Bang we find giant black holes, the harder they are to explain. "The closer to the Big Bang we find them, the faster their black holes must have grown or the more massive they must have been at birth — or both," Wolf said in the MPIA release.

There's a twist. Dún Aonghasa is feeding **slowly** right now, well below the theoretical speed limit for black hole growth (the Eddington limit). If it had always grown this slowly, it would have needed an extremely massive "seed" to start from, which current models struggle to explain. The team says it more likely grew in very rapid bursts earlier on. Their calculations fit a "direct collapse" idea, where a huge gas cloud collapses straight into a black hole. If even more distant quasars turn up, astronomers may need more exotic ideas, like black holes formed right after the Big Bang. Those remain hypothetical.

Euclid, launched in 2023, has now announced 31 ancient quasars in about a year and a half, including 12 beyond redshift 7. ESA says that's more than double what was known at that distance before.
