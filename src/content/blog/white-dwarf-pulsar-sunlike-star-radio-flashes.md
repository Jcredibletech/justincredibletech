Somewhere more than **5,000 light-years** away, something sends out a burst of radio waves every **7.9 minutes**, like a cosmic lighthouse. Astronomers now say they know what it is: a dead star, called a **white dwarf**, orbiting a star a lot like our own Sun. It's the first time this kind of pairing has been found.

## What they found

The object is called **GPM J071507−114153**. It belongs to a puzzling class of objects discovered only in the last few years called **long-period radio transients**, which flash radio waves on a schedule of minutes to hours.

Here's how the team, led by Sam McSweeney (who did the work at the Curtin University node of the International Centre for Radio Astronomy Research), pieced it together, according to [Curtin University](https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/media-release/researchers-discover-rare-star-system-that-emits-radio-signals-every-eight-minutes/) and co-author Natasha Hurley-Walker's [account in The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/a-dead-star-is-flashing-radio-waves-every-8-minutes-and-its-partner-looks-a-lot-like-the-sun-294665):

- In late 2024, the **Murchison Widefield Array** in Western Australia picked up flashes every 7.9 minutes.
- The flashes then vanished for weeks. Plotting every detection showed they appear only during a window of about **six hours**, then disappear for **26 hours**, which points to two stars orbiting each other every **32 hours**.
- The **MeerKAT** telescope in South Africa pinpointed the location, and the **Keck** telescope in Hawaii showed the visible star is a **G-type star**, nearly the same mass and temperature as the Sun.

## How they know it's a white dwarf

The pulses arrive up to **4 seconds early or late** depending on where the pulsing object is in its orbit. Combined with how little the Sun-like star wobbles, that lets the team weigh the unseen object at about **two-thirds the mass of the Sun**. That's too light to be a neutron star, so the team concludes it's a **white dwarf pulsar**.

"This is the first time the pulses are clear enough to see the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion," McSweeney said in Curtin's release.

As a bonus, the radio signals let the team spot **flares** on the Sun-like star and a likely **stellar prominence**, a big loop of plasma.

## Why it matters

White dwarfs sitting next to bright stars are very hard to see, because the bright star's glare hides them. Radio pulses cut through that glare, so surveys like this could reveal a hidden population. That matters because some white dwarf pairs are thought to end up as **Type Ia supernovae**, the exploding stars astronomers use to measure cosmic distances and the expansion of the universe. The team expects to find many more with the upcoming Square Kilometre Array telescopes.
