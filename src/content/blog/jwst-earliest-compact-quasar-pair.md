Two giant black holes, feeding side by side, less than a billion years after the Big Bang. That's what astronomers say they've found, and they admit they can't fully explain how it's possible.

## What they found

An international team led by **Silvia Onorato** of NSF NOIRLab reported the discovery of **J1602+4228AB**, nicknamed **Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua**. It's a pair of **quasars**, the blazing centers of galaxies where supermassive black holes are pulling in huge amounts of gas, which makes them shine brighter than all the stars around them.

According to [NOIRLab's release via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-earliest-compact-quasar-pair-poses.html) and [Space.com](https://www.space.com/astronomy/black-holes/astronomers-stunned-after-james-webb-space-telescope-discovers-the-earliest-pair-of-black-hole-powered-quasars):

- We see the pair as it was about **900 million years after the Big Bang**, when the universe was roughly a thirteenth of its current age.
- The two black holes weigh about **1.5 billion** and **400 million** times the mass of the Sun.
- They sit only about **18,100 light-years apart**, roughly one-fifth the width of the Milky Way.
- That makes it the **oldest and most compact quasar pair** found from the era astronomers call cosmic dawn. It is only the third quasar pair known from that period.

The name, created through the A Hua He Inoa program at the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center of Hawaiʻi, translates roughly to "two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the universe."

## How they found it

The first hint came from Webb's **Aether survey**, which used the telescope's NIRSpec instrument to study about 200 early quasars, around 43% of all known quasars from that era. While inspecting the data, the team noticed one quasar had a faint companion. Follow-up observations with the **Gemini North** telescope on Maunakea, Hawaiʻi, separated the two. Onorato said unusually clear skies let the team collect that data in just two nights.

The results were published in **The Astrophysical Journal Letters** (DOI: 10.3847/2041-8213/aeb11f).

## Why it matters

Current models say black holes need a long time to grow to billions of solar masses. "There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our universe, much less two," Onorato said.

The team's leading idea is that the pair marks an unusually dense knot of matter in the early universe, where colliding galaxies fed both star formation and rapid black hole growth. That's a proposal, not a settled answer. Either way, the system gives astronomers a rare natural laboratory for studying how the first massive galaxies and their black holes came together.
