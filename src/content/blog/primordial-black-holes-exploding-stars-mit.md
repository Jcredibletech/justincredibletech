What if a black hole the size of an atom got swallowed by a star? A new study says the star would lose, in a way we could see.

## What happened

Physicists led by **Ore Gottlieb**, an assistant professor at MIT, published a study on **November 19, 2026**, in the **Astrophysical Journal** titled "The Life and Death of Stars that Capture Primordial Black Holes" ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2026/if-tiny-primordial-black-holes-exist-heres-how-we-might-find-them-1119), [IOPscience](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/aeb7e1)). Co-authors are from the Flatiron Institute, NYU and Stanford.

**Primordial black holes** are hypothetical. Some physicists think they may have formed in the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang. If they exist, they could make up part of **dark matter**, the unseen stuff that makes up most of the universe's matter. None has been confirmed.

## What the simulations show

This is theory and computer simulation, not an observation. The team looked at black holes about the size of an atom but with the mass of an asteroid, small enough to be hard to spot but big enough to survive since the Big Bang. They found:

- A single star can't easily capture one, but a star with a **companion** (another star or a planet) could trap it over thousands to millions of years.
- Once trapped, the black hole sinks to the star's core and starts eating it from the inside.
- If the star spins fast enough, a disk forms around the black hole. "Once the disk forms, this is essentially a death sentence for the star," Gottlieb told MIT News.
- Jets then punch through the star and blow it apart **within a few dozen seconds**.

The explosion would give off **X-rays and gamma rays**, then **blue and ultraviolet light**, then optical, infrared and radio. The team says it should be bright enough for current telescopes, even from other galaxies.

## The catch

Other events can make similar flashes. Gottlieb said astronomers would need extra clues, such as a star exploding that is too small to explode on its own.

## Why it matters

Dark matter is one of the biggest open questions in physics. Even if no such flash is ever seen, the authors say the search can help rule primordial black holes in or out as part of dark matter.
