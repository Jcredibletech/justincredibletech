The most famous unsolved problem in mathematics may or may not have just fallen, and an AI model is at the center of the claim.

## What happened

On **Friday, November 20, 2026**, **Levent Alpöge**, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, posted a short note on his personal website, [alpo.ge](https://alpo.ge/), that begins: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/anthropic-mathematician-claims-to-prove-riemann-hypothesis-most-famous-problem-in-all-of-math/) reported the claim, and it quickly became the top story on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/).

According to Scientific American and a detailed write-up by [Mad Robot](https://madrobot.blog/2026/11/20/alpoge-claude-riemann-hypothesis-proof-claim/):

- The proof was posted as code in **Lean**, a programming language in which a computer checks every logical step. There is **no traditional paper** yet; the note says a readable write-up will come later.
- The note claims the method also proves the much broader **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis**.
- It gives Claude unusual credit, saying Claude "far outpaced me near the finish line."

## What is the Riemann Hypothesis?

Proposed by Bernhard Riemann in **1859**, it says that all the "nontrivial" zeros of a function called the Riemann zeta function lie on one line. If true, it means the prime numbers are spread out about as evenly as possible. Thousands of results in math assume it, and it's one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's **$1 million Millennium Prize Problems**.

## Why it's still a claim

When the claim first spread Friday morning, no outside mathematician had publicly confirmed the result, Mad Robot reported (see the update below). Lean code is strong evidence, but only of what it actually states. Experts now need to:

- build the code and confirm it runs,
- confirm the final theorem really is the Riemann Hypothesis and not a subtly different statement, and
- check that it doesn't rely on hidden extra assumptions.

Even if it holds up, the Clay Institute's rules require a solution to be published and generally accepted for at least two years before it considers a prize. Anthropic had not publicly commented, and the note itself acknowledges "an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing," a day after Anthropic's stock began trading, and says that view is false.

Alpöge has been involved in several of this year's AI math results, including a Claude-assisted result on the Jacobian conjecture that he posted in July, Scientific American noted.

## Update, Friday afternoon (Nov 20)

- **The code compiles, according to one outside expert.** MIT mathematician **Andrew Sutherland** told [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/anthropic-mathematician-claims-to-prove-riemann-hypothesis-most-famous-problem-in-all-of-math/) he had independently compiled Alpöge's result in Lean and called it "an historic moment," while adding that "there is much work to be done to understand the mathematical details of the proof." Mathematician Elliot Glazer posted that the formal statement in the release is in fact the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, [OfficeChai reported](https://officechai.com/ai/how-mathematicians-have-reacted-to-claimed-riemann-hypothesis-proof-by-anthropics-levent-alpoge/). A compile means the computer accepted the code; it does not mean anyone understands the argument yet.
- **It is enormous.** A footnote in Alpöge's note says a written-out version "hit 250,000+ pages," which is why he posted only the Lean code. Fields Medalist **Terence Tao** [called it](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/) an "(enormous) Lean-only proof" and said that, from very initial analysis, it appears to use the full power of modern work on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry. He expects "the digestion process will take some time."
- **A second Millennium Prize claim, same day.** Alpöge and NYU's **Tristan Buckmaster**, with heavy AI assistance per Tao, posted a 79-page paper and Lean code claiming that the Navier-Stokes fluid equations can blow up in finite time without any outside force, another of the Clay Institute's $1 million problems ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ai-just-solved-the-real-navier-stokes-problem/), [GitHub](https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/)). A key numerical "certificate" the result depends on has not been released yet, so outsiders can't fully check it.
- **A separate, all-human claim also surfaced.** Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil [posted](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/) a draft by Alessandro Masullo proposing a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis "with no AI," saying Brian Conrad has been checking it for months and it is "so far looking good." It is also unverified.

## Why it matters

If verified, this would be one of the biggest results in mathematics in generations, with an AI model doing much of the work. If it falls apart under review, it will be a reminder that big AI claims need independent checks. Either way, the next few days of expert review are what count.
