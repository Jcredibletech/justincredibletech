Send a text to Voyager 1 today and it won't arrive until tomorrow. Literally.

## What happened

On **Wednesday, November 18, 2026**, NASA's **Voyager 1** became the first human-made object to reach **one light-day** from Earth, the distance light travels in 24 hours. That's about **16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers)**, according to [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/voyager-one-light-day-downloads/). [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/voyager/1-light-day-from-earth-nasas-voyager-1-probe-hits-huge-milestone-in-interstellar-space) reported the milestone came at **5:16 a.m. EST**.

"The Earth and Voyager are exactly 24 hours apart at the speed of light," Voyager project scientist **Linda Spilker** told Space.com.

A light-day is a distance, not a time. Light covers about 186,000 miles every second. Voyager 1, moving at roughly **38,000 mph**, needed nearly 50 years to cover the same ground.

## What it's like to talk to it now

NASA talks to Voyager through the **Deep Space Network**, a set of giant radio dishes in California, Spain and Australia. Radio waves move at light speed, so a command now takes 24 hours to arrive, and the reply takes another 24 to come back.

"You need to have patience," Spilker told Space.com. Send a command, and two days later you learn whether it worked.

## A quick look back

Voyager 1 launched on **September 5, 1977**, about two weeks after its twin, Voyager 2, but on a faster path. Along the way it:

- found that Jupiter's moon **Io** has active volcanoes, and spotted a faint ring around Jupiter,
- found that Saturn's moon **Titan** has a thick atmosphere, and
- in **August 2012**, became the first spacecraft to cross the heliopause and enter **interstellar space**. Voyager 2 followed in 2018.

Both probes carry the famous **Golden Record**, with sounds and images from Earth. Some instruments have been shut off over the years to save power, but both still send back science data. Space.com reports the flight team is just eight to ten people.

## What's next

The mission turns **50** next year. Voyager 2 is on a slower path and won't reach one light-day until around **2035**, according to Space.com.

## Why it matters

Voyager 1 is still measuring cosmic rays, particles and radio waves in a region of space we'd otherwise only know from theory. And it shows what a well-built machine from the 1970s can still do.
