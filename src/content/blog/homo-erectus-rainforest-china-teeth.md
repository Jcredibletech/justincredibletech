For a long time, scientists pictured *Homo erectus* as a creature of open grasslands that faded away as forests took over. A handful of teeth gathered by ancient porcupines is challenging that picture.

## What happened

Two studies published in **Nature** on **November 18, 2026**, describe **nine hominin teeth** from **Naxian and Baxian caves**, about 20 kilometers north of Chongzuo in southern China's Guangxi region. **Eight** were identified as *Homo erectus*, according to [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11155-6), [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ancient-fossils-reveal-homo-erectus-survived-in-china-some-200-000-years-longer-than-we-thought/) and [ABC News (Australia)](https://www.abc.net.au/news/science/2026-11-19/other-ancient-humans-may-have-adapted-to-rainforests/107394402).

The team led in part by geochronologist **Kira Westaway** of Macquarie University dated the fossil layers with five independent methods to between **324,000 and 184,000 years ago**. Before this, the youngest securely dated *H. erectus* fossils on the Asian mainland were about **400,000 years old**.

The researchers think the teeth were collected by **porcupines**, which gnaw bones to sharpen their teeth and left the hardest parts behind.

## The rainforest clue

Chemical signatures in tooth enamel tell scientists what an animal ate and where:

- **Carbon isotopes** in the teeth of animals from the caves showed they fed in **closed-canopy forest**, and the *H. erectus* teeth carried similar signatures. That suggests these people lived in the rainforest, not just passed through.
- **Strontium** in the *H. erectus* teeth didn't match the local limestone. The researchers say that points to individuals who traveled widely, possibly from as far as northern **Vietnam**, about 250 kilometers away.

"They're very flexible, they're adaptable, and they're mobile," co-author Renaud Joannes-Boyau of Southern Cross University told the ABC.

## A mystery tooth

The **ninth tooth** didn't match *H. erectus*, Neanderthals or modern humans. A second team, led by Frido Welker of the University of Copenhagen, analyzed ancient enamel proteins and suggested Denisovans as "the first option to consider," while cautioning that there's no comparable Denisovan tooth to check against. The same protein work suggests the *H. erectus* teeth may come from a previously unknown lineage.

## The caveats

Outside experts were broadly positive but noted the limits. Griffith University palaeontologist Julien Louys, who wasn't involved, told the ABC that "the sample size for this conclusion is small," though if it holds up it would show unusual ecological flexibility.

## Why it matters

It suggests *Homo erectus* overlapped in time more closely with other human species in Asia than previously shown, and that adapting to rainforests wasn't unique to our own species.
