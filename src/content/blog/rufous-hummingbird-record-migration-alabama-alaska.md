A male **rufous hummingbird**, a bird that weighs a few grams, flew about **6,526 kilometers (4,055 miles)** from coastal **Alabama to Juneau, Alaska**, in **38 days**. That's the longest hummingbird migration ever tracked, according to a study published this month in [*Ecology and Evolution*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13774821/) and covered this week by [*Science*](https://www.science.org/content/article/tiny-tracking-device-documents-record-breaking-hummingbird-journey) and [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-tiny-tracker-reveals-longest-hummingbird.html).

## What happened

Hummingbirds are hard to follow because most trackers are too heavy for animals under 5 grams. A team led by avian biologist **Emma Rhodes** of the **University of Memphis** tested a new solar-powered Bluetooth transmitter called **BlūMorpho**. It weighs about **0.06 grams**, roughly a grain of rice.

In spring 2025, the researchers glued the tags to a small trimmed patch between the shoulders of **four rufous and seven ruby-throated hummingbirds** in coastal Alabama and Florida. Each tag weighed no more than 3% of the bird's body weight.

The clever part: the tags don't store data. They send a signal every three seconds, and when a compatible phone, tablet or computer comes within about 200 meters, it **anonymously relays the bird's location** to an online database. That let the team follow birds almost in real time.

## Key numbers

- **23,784** location points across **23 U.S. states, Mexico and Canada**
- About **47 days** of data per bird on average, up to **128 days**
- **6,526 km in 38 days** for the record-setting rufous hummingbird

The tags also caught some surprises. One rufous hummingbird flew north, then made a 345-km (214-mile) loop south before heading east. One ruby-throated hummingbird flew southeast into Florida instead of north.

## Why it matters

Rhodes told *Science* that migratory hummingbirds are in decline. Knowing exactly where they stop to refuel could help target conservation, like protecting the right habitat along their route. The same tag technology has been used on monarch butterflies.

The authors are clear about limits. The main constraint is how long a tag stays attached, not battery life. The study also "was not designed to quantify potential behavioral, physiological, or survival effects" of wearing the tags, so more research is needed on whether they affect the birds.
