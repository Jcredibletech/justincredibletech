Mount Everest stands almost 9 kilometers (about 29,000 feet) tall. For much of Earth's history, mountains that high weren't even possible. A new study says mountains got taller in two sudden "growth spurts," and that those jumps line up with two of the biggest turning points for life on our planet.

## What the researchers found

The study, ["Raising mountains for a habitable Earth,"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aei2764) was published in *Science Advances* and announced on November 13 by the [University of British Columbia](https://science.ubc.ca/news/2026-11/mountain-growth-spurts-may-have-sparked-complex-life-earth) and [Utrecht University](https://www.uu.nl/en/news/did-mountains-make-a-habitable-world). The lead author is Matthijs Smit of UBC, working with Douwe van Hinsbergen of Utrecht, Ellen Kooijman of the Swedish Museum of Natural History, and others.

The team studied **metamorphic rocks**, rocks that were pushed deep underground where tectonic plates collided and then later came back to the surface. Those rocks record how thick the crust under ancient mountain ranges once was. Thicker crust means taller mountains.

Their results show two jumps rather than a slow, steady climb:

- **Around 2.3 billion years ago**, the thickest mountain crust went from about 30 km to 60 km, and maximum mountain height rose from roughly 1 km to 4.5 km.
- **Around 800 million years ago**, it jumped again, to almost 90 km of crust and peaks up to about 9 km, Everest territory.

## Why mountains would matter for life

Mountains wear down. Rain and erosion grind them into sediment that washes into the oceans, carrying nutrients like **phosphorus** that life needs. "Prior to three billion years ago, these elements were locked in the Earth's crust and the oceans were quite nutrient-starved," Smit said in UBC's release. "All this changed when tall mountains started to develop."

The timing is the eye-catcher. The first spurt lines up with the first major rise of oxygen in the atmosphere, and the second with another big oxygen increase around 700 to 800 million years ago, not long before complex animal life took off. Utrecht's release adds that heavy weathering pulled CO2 out of the air, and long ice ages followed both spurts.

As for why mountains suddenly could grow taller, the researchers suspect Earth's slow **cooling** made the crust stiffer and able to hold more weight.

## The caveats

This is a correlation in deep time, and the researchers frame it that way. Kooijman said the link is "most likely no coincidence," and van Hinsbergen said mountains "may well have played a far greater role" in making Earth habitable "than previously assumed." The study suggests mountains helped set the stage; it doesn't prove they were the single trigger for complex life.

## Why it matters

It's a reminder that life's story isn't only biology. Plate tectonics and the shape of the land may have controlled when Earth's air and oceans became friendly to complex life. That also helps scientists judge which rocky planets elsewhere might be habitable.
