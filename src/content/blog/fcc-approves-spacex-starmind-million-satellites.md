The **Federal Communications Commission** has approved **SpaceX's "Starmind"** plan to put up to **1 million satellites** in orbit that would work as **data centers in space**. FCC Chairman **Brendan Carr** announced the decision on **Wednesday, Nov. 25**, calling it the first authorization in U.S. history for a constellation of orbital data centers. On **Saturday, Nov. 28**, Elon Musk added fuel to the story, posting on X that Google wants to rent more computing capacity from SpaceX.

## What happened

According to the [FCC's order](https://eu-assets.contentstack.com/v3/assets/blt23eb5bbc4124baa6/bltf4cdf2af47159e12/6b071c5f5506bb38bca5b0ec/FCC_report_and_order_on_SpaceX_Starmind_AI_constellation_11-25-2026.pdf), the satellites would fly in shells between about **500 and 1,002 km** (310 to 620 miles) up. The license comes with deadlines:

- **1 satellite** operating by **Nov. 25, 2033**
- **10%** of the constellation by **2035**
- **50%** by **2038**
- All **1 million** by **2040**

The FCC **deferred** one key request: SpaceX wanted permission to send satellites above 600 km into "graveyard" or solar orbits instead of burning them up in the atmosphere. The FCC said SpaceX hasn't given enough detail yet and must come back with a plan before launching any satellites that won't re-enter.

SpaceX says Starmind would use solar power in orbit to run AI computing, sending data to Earth through laser links to **Starlink**, [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1042278/spacex-fcc-starmind-ai-space-data-centers) reported. Carr said on X that Starmind and competing constellations "will use solar power to extend America's lead" in AI. Musk replied, "Much appreciated."

## Who objected

The approval wasn't unopposed. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/25/spacex-fcc-satellite-approval.html) reported that **Amazon** (which runs the rival Leo satellite network), **Viasat** and the **American Astronomical Society** filed petitions to deny it. Amazon called Starmind a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan." Astronomers worry about brighter night skies and space debris, and an Earthjustice attorney criticized the FCC for skipping an environmental review. The FCC said environmental and optical-astronomy concerns fall outside its authority.

## Why it matters

AI data centers on the ground are running into power shortages and local opposition. Putting them in orbit, where sunlight is constant, is the bet SpaceX, Google (Project Suncatcher) and Blue Origin are all chasing. Musk's Saturday post, reported by [CNBC/Reuters](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/28/musk-says-google-wants-to-rent-more-computing-capacity-from-spacex.html), came without details. Google already pays SpaceX **$920 million a month** for ground-based computing under a deal running through June 2029. Skeptics quoted by the [AP](https://apnews.com/article/spacex-data-center-elon-musk-b9897fe06f851ffaf92fd304ae69075c) point out a physics problem: space is cold, but a vacuum traps heat, so getting rid of the heat from AI chips is hard and expensive. We've covered SpaceX's [Starship and Starlink V3](/blog/starship-flight-14-orbital-starlink-v3/) and its recent [BEAD grant decision](/blog/spacex-declines-bead-grants-free-starlink-hardware/).

## What's next

SpaceX hasn't said when the first Starmind satellite will launch. It still needs to file a disposal plan, and opponents could ask the full Commission to review the Space Bureau's decision.
