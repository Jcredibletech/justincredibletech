If you or someone you know is on Medicaid, here's a change to the new work requirement. On Friday, November 13, the **Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS)** announced that states can choose an optional "hold harmless" period through **June 30, 2027**.

## What the rule is

The work requirement comes from last year's budget law, H.R. 1, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Starting **January 1, 2027**, certain Medicaid applicants and enrollees ages **19 to 64** must meet it unless they are excluded. Under CMS's June 2026 rule, that means one of these:

- 80 hours a month of work, community service or a work program
- enrolling in school at least half-time
- a combination of those totaling 80 hours a month
- meeting a monthly income threshold

There are exclusions, including for people who are medically frail or disabled.

## What changed Friday

According to the [CMS announcement](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/cms-announces-hold-harmless-period-strengthen-medicaid-work-requirement-systems-improve-beneficiary), states that opt in will still **check** whether people meet the requirement or are excluded starting January 1. But until June 30, 2027, they **will not deny applications or disenroll otherwise eligible people solely** for not meeting it. States can also use a shorter grace period or skip it, and any state that wants it is supposed to notify CMS by **December 11**, [HFMA reports](https://www.hfma.org/payment-reimbursement-and-managed-care/medicaid-work-requirements-2027-enforcement/).

CMS says the extra time is for states to modernize systems, train staff and improve the experience for enrollees. "If the state can use data to show that someone is working or qualifies for an exclusion, that person should not have to track down paperwork to prove it," said Dan Brillman, who directs CMS's Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services. CMS also urged states to verify work automatically using data they already have and to send clear notices.

## The debate around it

Supporters of the policy see work requirements as a way to connect enrollees to jobs and training; CMS calls this "paths to prosperity." Critics, including state Medicaid administrators, hospitals, insurers and physician groups, have warned about paperwork burdens and tight "medical frailty" rules, [Fierce Healthcare reports](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/trump-administration-delays-enforcement-medicaid-work-requirements-six-months). Fierce notes a coalition of 26 states sued over the rollout, a Massachusetts judge sided with the states on the medical frailty definition in October, and studies estimate 5 million to 10 million people could lose coverage in 2028 once the requirements are in effect.

## What it means for you

Nothing about the rule itself changed, and it's up to each state whether to use the grace period. If you're on Medicaid and between 19 and 64, watch for notices from your state Medicaid agency and keep your contact information current. This article is general information, not personal advice; your state agency can tell you how the rule applies to you.
