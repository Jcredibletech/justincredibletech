If you use Facebook on an Android phone, you've probably seen it suggest photos from your camera roll as ideas for a post. Italy's privacy regulator says that convenience came at a cost, and on Saturday, November 14, it fined Meta **€805 million (about $887 million)** over it.

## What happened

Italy's data protection authority, the [Garante per la protezione dei dati personali](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10321080), said Facebook's Android app gained access to users' photos, videos and their metadata through a gallery-suggestions feature without getting specific, informed consent.

According to the regulator, as reported by the [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwn83gxemk35o) and [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/14/italy-fines-meta-887-million-over-android-users-data-access.html):

- Facebook asked for gallery access through **one app-wide permission**, and the notice before it didn't make clear that this would also let Facebook generate post suggestions from the gallery.
- About **20 million Android users in Italy** were affected.
- Meta has **60 days** to switch the feature off by default and ask for consent separately.
- The fine works out to roughly **0.47% of Meta's global revenue**. The regulator also cited the number of people affected, the fact that the feature was still running for some users, and what it called Meta's "lack of co-operation."

The BBC notes this is among the largest fines an Italian authority has ever imposed on a tech company.

## What Meta says

Meta plans to appeal. A spokesperson told the BBC: "We strongly disagree with the [regulator's] assessment of the facts, its application of the law and the disproportionate level of the fine. This is a straightforward feature which helps people post photos and videos to Facebook. People must give permission on their device before it can operate and they can change this permission at any time via their settings."

## What the case did and didn't cover

Two details are worth knowing, per CNBC's report on the regulator's statement:

- The investigation, which began in 2025, **did not look at whether the content was used to train AI models**. Meta denied doing that.
- The regulator reserved the right to look separately at a **newer feature launched in 2026** that lets gallery content be processed in the cloud.

## Why it matters

Europe's privacy rules generally require that consent be specific to each purpose, not bundled into one broad permission. This decision applies that idea to one of the most common things phone apps ask for: access to your photo library. If the ruling stands, it could push apps toward asking separately before they do anything beyond what you'd expect from a permission, especially in Europe. For now, Meta's appeal means the final outcome could take a while.

Want to check your own phone? On Android, you can review which apps can see your photos under Settings, then Apps, then Permissions.
