How long should it take to get government approval for a new power line, pipeline or power plant? A big bipartisan bill aimed at speeding that up cleared another hurdle in the Senate on Monday night.

## What happened

On **Monday, November 16, 2026**, the Senate voted **70-27** to invoke cloture, which limits debate, on **Sen. Shelley Moore Capito's substitute amendment** to **H.R. 2347**, the legislative vehicle for the permitting reform bill, according to the [Senate Press Gallery](https://www.dailypress.senate.gov/monday-november-16-2026/). [Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/bloomberg-government-news/permitting-overhaul-measure-advances-in-bipartisan-senate-vote) also reported the tally. Cloture needs 60 votes.

This was a **procedural vote, not final passage**. The substitute is the bill's final text, released **November 7** by its four lead negotiators: Sens. Capito (R-W.Va.), Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), Mike Lee (R-Utah) and Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.), per the [Bipartisan Policy Center](https://bipartisanpolicy.org/explainer/explaining-the-senate-permitting-bill-the-bipartisan-american-affordability-and-jobs-act/).

## What the bill would do

According to the Bipartisan Policy Center and [Heatmap News](https://heatmap.news/energy/managers-amendment-policy-permitting-bill):

- **Environmental reviews:** shorten the window to sue over reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) from the current **six years to 150 days**, and narrow who can sue.
- **Power lines:** require grid regions to plan long-distance lines with their neighbors under **FERC** oversight, with a formula so customers who benefit pay for them.
- **Federal backstop:** let the federal government approve a power line's route if a state refuses. Under the final text, developers must go through the state process for a year first.
- **Data centers:** require data centers to pay the added grid costs they create, so other customers aren't charged for them, and set up an Energy Department program to give local governments information on proposed data centers.

## What supporters and critics say

Supporters, including both parties' lead negotiators, argue faster approvals will get energy projects built and help bring down power bills. "Americans can't afford to wait for lower electricity prices. We need to pass this bill now," Heinrich said, as reported by the [AP via OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/11/09/senate-advances-sweeping-energy-legislation-in-first-major-action-since-midterm-elections/).

Critics on both sides remain. The Wilderness Society opposes the bill. "Americans deserve permitting reform that isn't crafted behind closed doors and rushed to the Senate floor," its president, Tracy Stone-Manning, said, per Heatmap. Some former Republican regulators had objected to earlier provisions on state authority, which the final text changed. President Trump has called the bill a win for energy but said he won't approve wind turbines, according to the same AP report.

## What happens next

The Senate still has to adopt the amendment and pass the bill, with a final vote expected before the Thanksgiving recess. Then it goes to the **House**, where Democratic Leader **Hakeem Jeffries**, expected to become speaker in January, has said the Senate deal "falls short" in several areas and wants more House input, [Politico](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2026/11/05/congress/jeffries-opposes-senate-permitting-deal-demands-more-house-input-01153083) reported. If the House doesn't act during the lame-duck session, the bill would have to start over in the new Congress.

## Why it matters to you

Electricity demand is rising fast, partly because of data centers. This bill is Congress's biggest attempt in years to change how quickly the grid and energy projects can be built, and who pays for it. That could affect both your power bill and how much say your community has over projects nearby.
