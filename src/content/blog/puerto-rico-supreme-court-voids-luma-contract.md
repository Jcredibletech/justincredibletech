Puerto Rico's Supreme Court ruled on **Wednesday, Nov. 25** that the island's government can remove **LUMA Energy**, the private company that has run Puerto Rico's power transmission and distribution grid since 2021. In a **5-4** decision, the court found that agreements with LUMA hadn't been properly approved, and gave the government **one year** to put a new operator in place, according to [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/puerto-rico-court-quashes-grid-contract-in-win-for-governor) and [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/26/nx-s1-6054139/court-rules-puerto-ricos-governor-can-fire-company-that-runs-troubled-power-grid).

## What happened

After Hurricane Maria wrecked the grid in 2017, Puerto Rico privatized power delivery. LUMA, a consortium based in Canada and Texas, got a 15-year deal and took over in 2021, promising a stronger grid and more reliable service. Blackouts have continued, and Gov. **Jenniffer González-Colón** filed suit last year to cancel the contract.

The court ruled on **procedural** grounds, not on LUMA's performance. Bloomberg Law reports the justices found a supplemental agreement between the government and LUMA never received the required board approval from the island's public-private partnership agency. The court also said LUMA is legally obligated to take part in an "orderly transfer" of operations over the next year.

## What each side says

**The governor:** "Today is a day of celebration on the island," González-Colón said, according to NPR. She said LUMA has "not fulfilled the requirements of the contract," pointing to continued outages, and that a public bid for a new private operator will start immediately. "It's going to be a good transition. We're going to make it in a year," she said.

**LUMA:** The company said it is considering its legal options, NPR reports, and has long argued it inherited a grid in terrible condition and isn't fully to blame for the blackouts. It has **10 days** to ask the court to reconsider.

**The oversight board:** The federal financial oversight board Congress created for Puerto Rico criticized the governor for ending the contract without a replacement ready, saying it's worried about continuity of service, according to NPR. Any new operator needs approval from both the local government and that board.

## Why it matters

About 3.2 million Americans live in Puerto Rico, and reliable power touches everything from hospitals to small businesses. The ruling hands the governor a major win, but also a deadline: find, approve and hand off to a new grid operator within a year, without making service worse in the meantime. It's a test of whether a government can swap out a private utility operator mid-contract.
