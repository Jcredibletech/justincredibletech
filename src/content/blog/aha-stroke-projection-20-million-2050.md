The number of U.S. adults living after a stroke is projected to nearly double, from about **10 million to almost 20 million by 2050**, according to a new **American Heart Association** scientific statement published **November 23** in its journal [*Stroke*](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/STR.0000000000000539). The yearly economic cost of stroke could climb more than fivefold, from about **$65 billion to nearly $423 billion**.

## What this is (and isn't)

This is a **projection, not a new clinical trial**. An AHA writing group led by neurologist **Hugo J. Aparicio** of Boston University took national survey data on who reports having had a stroke (2015-2020) and on health spending (2015-2019), then combined it with U.S. Census population projections. The AHA notes that scientific statements summarize what's known; they **don't make treatment recommendations**. The forecasts also leave out things that could change the picture, such as wider use of GLP-1 obesity and diabetes drugs, mobile stroke units and AI-assisted care.

## Key numbers

- Share of adults living with stroke: **3.9% in 2020 to a projected 6.4% in 2050**, per the [AHA](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/number-of-u-s-adults-living-with-stroke-projected-to-double-to-nearly-20-million-by-2050)
- Adults **20 to 44** see the **largest relative increase**; their annual stroke costs could grow from $7.9 billion to $84.4 billion
- Adults **45 to 64** would account for the biggest share of costs, about **$142 billion a year**
- By 2050, an estimated **10.4 million women and 9 million men** would be living after a stroke
- The fastest growth is projected among **Asian and Hispanic adults**. Black, American Indian and Alaska Native, and multiracial adults are projected to keep the highest prevalence.

## Why the numbers rise

Part of the increase is actually good news. Individual stroke risk and stroke deaths have fallen for decades, partly because fewer people smoke and more control their blood pressure. Aparicio said three things will still push totals up: an **older population**, **more people surviving** strokes and living with their effects, and **rising costs** of imaging, treatment and rehab. He said rising rates in younger adults "may reflect the earlier onset of high blood pressure, obesity and diabetes."

"These projections are not inevitable," Aparicio said in the AHA release. [Boston University](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/camed/news-events/articles/2026/by-2050-stroke-could-impact-20-million-americans-costing-423-billion-annually/) adds that the cost estimates don't include unpaid family caregiving, so the real burden is likely higher.

## Why it matters

The statement is aimed at health systems and policymakers, calling for earlier prevention, especially for younger adults and the communities facing the steepest increases. The AHA's general guidance is to know your blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol. This article isn't medical advice, so talk to a doctor about your own risk.
