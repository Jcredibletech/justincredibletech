In 2000, the United States reached a public health milestone: measles was officially "eliminated," meaning the virus was no longer spreading continuously in the country. According to multiple reports this weekend, that status is now gone.

## What happened

The [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/health/2026/11/13/us-loses-measles-elimination-status-erasing-landmark-achievement/), [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/public-health/us-measles-elimination-status-lost-outbreaks-continuous-spread-year-paho-cdc/) and The Wall Street Journal (as cited by [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/measles-associated-deaths-reach-10-in-us-after-two-more-deaths-reported)) reported, citing people familiar with the deliberations, that the **Pan American Health Organization (PAHO)** concluded this week that measles has spread **without interruption for more than 12 months** in the U.S. PAHO, the World Health Organization's regional office for the Americas, had planned a public announcement for **November 17**, so the formal statement hasn't been released yet.

"Elimination" doesn't mean zero cases. It means outbreaks brought in from abroad die out within a year. KFF reports PAHO based its decision on analyses by the CDC and an independent expert group, and that researchers expected this after an outbreak in Utah passed the one-year mark in June. The current wave began with a large outbreak in West Texas in January 2025.

## The numbers

- More than **5,400 confirmed cases** so far in 2026, more than double the **2,289** confirmed in 2025, per CDC and Johns Hopkins data cited by Al Jazeera.
- More than **830 hospitalizations** over the past two years, per KFF.
- Death counts differ by source. States have reported more measles-associated deaths than the CDC's national count. On Friday, **Pennsylvania and Wisconsin** each reported another death, one of them an infant.
- Kindergarten vaccination coverage fell from **95.2%** in the 2019-20 school year to **92.4%** in 2025-26, per Al Jazeera. About 95% is generally considered the level needed to stop outbreaks.

Two doses of the MMR (measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine are about **97% effective** at preventing measles, according to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/vaccines/index.html). Babies usually can't get their first dose until about age one, which is why outbreaks are especially risky for infants.

## What officials are saying

Department of Health and Human Services spokesperson Emily Hilliard attributed the resurgence to vaccination gaps that opened years before the current administration, KFF reports. White House spokesperson Allison Schuster said, "The President and the Secretary have repeatedly encouraged measles vaccination, coordinated with states, and facilitated the deployment of vaccine doses across the country." HHS also said it has provided about $100 million in additional outbreak funding.

Some public health experts quoted by KFF argue federal messaging and funding cuts weakened the response, which HHS disputes.

## What comes next

To get elimination status back, the current outbreaks have to end, and future outbreaks must be stopped within a year. The U.S. isn't alone: Canada, Spain and the United Kingdom have also lost the status recently.
