If your child has ever had a head CT, this study will get your attention. The key is reading the numbers carefully.

## What happened

A study published **November 19, 2026**, in **JAMA**, led by researchers at **UC Davis** and **UC San Francisco**, looked at more than **3.7 million children** in the U.S. and Ontario, Canada, born from 1996 to 2016, and followed them from birth for an average of **12.3 years**, according to [UC Davis Health](https://health.ucdavis.edu/news/headlines/children-exposed-to-more-radiation-from-medical-scans-may-face-higher-brain-tumor-risk/2026/11) and [Medical Daily](https://www.medicaldaily.com/childhood-ct-radiation-brain-tumor-risk-jama-481485). Researchers estimated each child's cumulative radiation dose to the brain from X-rays, CT scans, fluoroscopy and nuclear medicine exams.

## What they found

- **1,536** children were diagnosed with a brain tumor during follow-up; **82.5%** of those were classified as aggressive.
- Radiation equal to **one head CT** was associated with a **1.8-fold** higher relative risk of a brain tumor.
- In absolute terms, that's about **8 additional brain tumors per 10,000 children by age 21**, or roughly **one extra tumor per 1,266 head CTs**.
- Risk rose as cumulative dose went up, with a stronger link for aggressive tumors.
- The researchers estimated about **10%** of childhood and teen brain tumors in the study could be attributed to imaging radiation.

Most kids in the study never had a head CT: **4.3%** had one, **0.9%** had two and **0.4%** had three or more.

## What stage this research is at

This is an **observational cohort study**. It found an **association** and a dose-response pattern, but it **cannot prove** the scans caused the tumors, Medical Daily notes. Children who need head CTs may already have conditions that differ from children who don't. The figures are population estimates, not a prediction for any one child. The findings **do not change current medical guidance**.

## What the researchers say

"Our findings do not mean that parents should avoid getting their children medically necessary imaging. These examinations can be lifesaving," said first author **Diana Miglioretti** of UC Davis Health. Her message: every scan using ionizing radiation should be clinically justified and use the lowest dose that still gets the needed information.

Senior author **Rebecca Smith-Bindman**, a UCSF radiologist, said head CT "is invaluable in the right setting," but "it is overutilized."

## Why it matters

Head CTs are common in kids, especially after bumps and falls. The study supports careful use of imaging, not avoiding it. Questions about whether a specific scan is needed, or whether a non-radiation test could work, are ones to raise with your child's doctor, and no one should delay emergency care to ask them.
