This one isn't a gadget story, but it's the biggest news in the world this morning, so here's what's been confirmed so far. This is a developing story, and numbers may change.

## What happened

Just before **9 a.m. local time on Wednesday, October 28**, two high-speed trains collided on the **Madrid-Seville line** in **Toledo province**, central Spain, near the town of **Urda**. Both trains were heading south from Madrid in the same direction.

According to AP, The Guardian, El País and RTVE, a **Renfe AVE** train (Spain's state-owned operator) ran into the back of an **Iryo** train (a private operator) that was **stopped on the tracks**. Carriages from both trains left the track, and some went down an embankment.

## The toll so far

- The Castilla-La Mancha regional government says **at least 64 people have died**.
- **More than 500 passengers** were on the two trains. El País, citing the regional government, puts it at **544**: 147 on the Iryo service and 397 on the Renfe AVE.
- Regional leader Emiliano García-Page warned that the toll could rise, because rescuers had **not yet reached the bottom of some carriages**, according to The Guardian.

Spain's prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, said emergency services are on the ground and offered support to victims and their families. High-speed service between Madrid and Andalusia has been **suspended**.

## What we don't know yet

The big one: **why it happened**. The Guardian reports authorities have said the cause is **not yet known**. Spanish outlets are reporting early, **unconfirmed** findings about the line's signaling, but officials haven't confirmed a cause and an investigation is underway. I'm not going to guess. I'll update when investigators say something official.

## Why it matters

It's Spain's **second deadly high-speed rail crash this year**. In **January**, a collision near **Adamuz** involving trains from the same two companies killed **46 people**. Spain has one of the biggest high-speed rail networks in the world, and the Madrid-Seville line is its oldest. So expect hard questions about rail safety there in the weeks ahead.
