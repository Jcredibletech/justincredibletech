Even the spies have a "we've detected unusual activity" week.

The CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) discovered a breach of an unclassified computer system that analysts use to study public and commercially available information. The New York Times first reported the intrusion on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, and the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/cia-cyberattack-unclassified-system-bfa6d3b02e3b0e2779dfb613c900bd77), [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/24/hackers-breach-cia-unclassified-computer-system/), [Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/11/cia-odni-unclassified-intelligence-system-hacked/417438/) and [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/cia-odni-osint-data-breach/) each confirmed it with their own sources.

## What was hit

The affected system supports open-source intelligence, or OSINT. That's the unclassified side of spying: news reports, social media, and paid data such as commercial satellite imagery, which analysts use to follow foreign governments and military activity.

"The hack did not involve our classified networks or classified information, and no other systems are currently at risk," a source told CyberScoop. A person who spoke to AP said the issue has been resolved. A source told [GovInfoSecurity](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/cia-dni-probe-hack-unclassified-intelligence-system-a-33514) the intrusion "exposed some unclassified data."

The CIA declined to comment. ODNI did not respond to requests for comment from Nextgov and CyberScoop on Tuesday.

## Why "unclassified" still matters

Security experts say the raw data isn't the secret; what the agencies do with it is. "If you can see what analysts are searching for and what topics they're tracking, you get a solid look at U.S. intelligence priorities and blind spots without ever touching a classified file," Ben Bernstein of the security firm Huntress told Nextgov and CyberScoop. He added that OSINT systems are hard to lock down because they have to stay connected to the public internet to do their job.

## Who did it?

That hasn't been officially confirmed. Nextgov reports that ARTEX, an AI tool that automatically finds and breaks into computer systems and has become popular with criminal hackers, is believed to have been used. A New York Times reporter posted that early indications suggest China may be responsible, GovInfoSecurity reported; no agency has publicly named an attacker. The Times also reported that the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency was affected at the same time.

## Part of a rough month for federal systems

This comes days after officials acknowledged a hack of the FBI's National Crime Information Center, the database police use to check warrants and criminal records ([our earlier story](/blog/fbi-ncic-crime-database-hack/)). The Agriculture Department also took several applications offline after a security incident that disrupted tools used for payments to farmers, Nextgov reported.

What we still don't know:

- Exactly what data was exposed or copied.
- How long the intruders had access.
- Who was behind it.

We'll update this story if the CIA or ODNI says more.
