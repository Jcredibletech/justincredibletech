The internet's favorite AI story today has a twist ending. A Google engineer pointed 48 AI agents at NASA laser scans of the Mexican jungle, and they flagged what looked like a Maya pyramid nobody had put on a map. It shot to the top of Hacker News and spread across X. Then archaeologists pointed out that the site had already been published.

## What happened

- **The experiment.** Pietro Miotti, a Google engineer who says plainly he's not an archaeologist, wrote up the project on his blog. On October 18 he wondered what explorer Hiram Bingham could have done with today's tools. By that night he had AI agents analyzing public lidar data over the Maya region ([Pietro Miotti](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site/)).
- **How the agents worked.** Lidar fires laser pulses through gaps in the tree canopy to map the bare ground underneath. The agents turned about 1,200 square kilometers of NASA lidar strips into images, and 48 of them went through those images one by one, looking for pyramids, platforms and plazas. Miotti says there was no special model or classifier: the agents looked at the pictures and followed a written guide.
- **The "find."** One hilltop stood out: a leveled plaza, an oval pyramid at least 8 to 12 meters tall, a raised courtyard and a hollow that might have been a water reservoir. Miotti reported it to Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH), and on October 21 INAH replied that it had no registered archaeological site at that location. From idea to that reply took less than 72 hours ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/google-researcher-discovers-unmapped-maya-site-in-mexico-using-48-ai-agents-project-takes-less-than-72-hours-after-agents-scour-nasa-lidar-data)).
- **The twist.** As the story went viral, archaeologist Sarah Parcak said the site had been published by Tulane's Francisco Estrada-Belli ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1031498/an-ai-agent-swarm-may-have-unearthed-an-unmapped-maya-site)). On November 11 Miotti updated his post: the site appears in a 2025 lidar study by Estrada-Belli and colleagues ([Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2025.105288)). The agents missed it while searching the literature, so they rediscovered it rather than discovered it. It just wasn't in INAH's register yet.

## Why it matters to you

The tools here aren't exotic. The data is public, and the agents are the same kind of AI many people already pay for. That means regular people can now take on research projects that used to need a lab, a grant and years of training. The flip side is just as clear: AI can be great at spotting patterns and still miss the one paper that matters, and "not in a government database" is not the same as "unknown."

## My take

I love this story, twist and all. A hobbyist with a laptop got a real result in a weekend, and the experts caught the mistake within days. That's the system working. I'd rather see more people building and testing ideas like this than see anyone gatekeep who gets to use these tools. Just check the literature, and let the archaeologists do the digging. That's my opinion; the facts above come from Miotti's write-up, the 2025 study and the outlets linked.
