The European Space Agency's **Hera** spacecraft has sent back its first close-up images of **Dimorphos**, the small asteroid NASA deliberately hit with its **DART** spacecraft in 2022. ESA unveiled them on **Wednesday, Nov. 25**, at Berlin's Museum für Naturkunde. The headline finding: the impact flattened one side of Dimorphos into a crater about the size of a sports stadium, stretching across the asteroid's entire width.

## What happened

On Sept. 26, 2022, DART slammed into Dimorphos, a **151-meter** moonlet that orbits the **730-meter** asteroid Didymos about 1 kilometer away. Telescopes on Earth showed the hit shortened Dimorphos' orbit by **33 minutes**, much more than predicted. What nobody could see was what happened to the asteroid itself.

Hera arrived this month to find out. According to [ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-reveals-stadium-sized-asteroid-crater-left-after-spacecraft-impact), its early images show:

- A **flattened face** with an impact crater as wide as the struck side, including an odd cigar-shaped structure in the middle.
- The rest of Dimorphos looks basically **unchanged**, with boulder fields matching DART's 2022 images.
- Dimorphos is now **tumbling wildly** in its orbit.
- **Hundreds of previously unknown boulders**, each just a few meters across, circling the pair.

"Scientists debated whether Dimorphos had sustained an impact crater, changed its shape or stayed the same," Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel said in ESA's release. "Much to our surprise the answer turns out to be all three at once!"

The lead image was taken on Nov. 7 from **29 km** away, at about 2.7 meters per pixel. The [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cw8dv112r5gmo) also reported the stadium-sized crater finding from the Berlin briefing.

## Why it matters

DART was humanity's first real test of nudging an asteroid by ramming it. It proved an orbit can be changed. Hera shows *how* the target responded, which helps scientists predict how a future threatening asteroid would react to the same technique. "By combining these two missions, humanity is taking a decisive step towards making asteroid deflection a reliable tool for protecting our planet," said Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli.

## What's next

Hera, about the size of a small car, is flying 25 to 30 km from the asteroids at just 12 centimeters per second. ESA says the team will move in closer for higher-resolution images in the coming weeks, and will need to account for the newly found boulders as it does. Hera also carries two small CubeSats, Milani and Juventas, built to study the asteroids up close.
