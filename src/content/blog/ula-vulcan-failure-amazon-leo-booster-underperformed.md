Sunday was supposed to be another step in Amazon's plan to build a satellite internet network to rival Starlink. Instead, 40 of its satellites were lost when United Launch Alliance's Vulcan rocket failed to reach orbit. On Tuesday, ULA shared its first clues about what went wrong.

## What happened

A **Vulcan** rocket lifted off from **Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral, Florida**, on **Sunday, November 15, 2026**, carrying **40 Amazon Leo satellites**. ULA [confirmed](https://blog.ulalaunch.com/blog/vulcan-leo-1-updates) that afternoon that the rocket "suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit." [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/vulcan-launch-of-amazon-leo-satellites-fails/) also reported the failure.

## What ULA says now

In an update posted at **9:30 a.m. EST on Tuesday, November 17**, ULA said "early indications" are that, just after liftoff, the rocket "experienced low booster performance." That meant the stages separated while the rocket was too low and below the minimum speed needed to reach orbit. Key points from ULA's update:

- The **Centaur second stage** "appears to have started nominally," and its **RL10 engines** ran as planned, but it couldn't make up for the lost performance.
- The **solid rocket boosters** strapped to the side appear to have performed normally, following nozzle improvements made for this launch.
- The **FAA** will oversee the ULA-led investigation and must approve the final report and any fixes.

"It is very early in the process, and we want to focus on the data and avoid speculation until the investigation is complete," ULA said. The company has not identified a root cause or a return-to-flight date.

## The engine question

Vulcan's first stage is powered by **BE-4 engines** made by Blue Origin, the same engine type used on Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket. [WESH](https://www.wesh.com/article/ula-vulcan-booster-underperformed-loss-amazon-satellite/74453556) notes a BE-4 oxygen valve problem caused a New Glenn to explode on its pad in May, but there is **no established link** between that and Sunday's failure. "It might have been something to do with the engine and it may not have been," aerospace engineer Marshall Kaplan of the University of Maryland told WESH. Experts quoted by the station said investigations like this can take weeks to months.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said on X that NASA is available to help with the investigation.

## Why it matters

Vulcan is one of the main rockets the U.S. relies on for national security launches, so a pause while it's investigated squeezes launch capacity. It's also a setback for Amazon Leo, which needs many launches to fill out its constellation, and it arrives the same weekend SpaceX caught a Starship upper stage for the first time. For people waiting on Amazon's satellite internet, the timeline now depends on how fast ULA finds and fixes the problem.
