Your gut is home to trillions of microbes, and scientists just found a whole new group of viruses living among them, one that may solve a long-running mystery about where the smallpox family came from.

## What they found

In a paper published in **Nature** this week, a team led by researchers at **Genoscope** in France and the **French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS)** describes a previously unknown group of large DNA viruses they named **egoviruses** ([Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11283-z)). Nature's news team covered the study on November 18, and [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-newly-gut-viruses-clues-smallpox.html) followed on November 19.

The scientists never had to collect a single new sample. They searched more than **85,000 existing DNA datasets** from the environment and animal digestive systems, looking for one gene common in large DNA viruses. According to [Nature's news report](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03766-w) and Phys.org:

- They assembled **12 complete egovirus genomes** and found egovirus markers in **864 samples**, almost all from digestive systems.
- Egoviruses turned up in people, **buffalo, pigs, goats, chickens and deer**. They were in about **26% of buffalo** samples, **11% of pig** samples and **2.3% of human** samples.
- One human group of egoviruses, found in people in Europe, Asia, Africa and North America, is estimated to be in **roughly 1% of the world's population**. Nature's news story puts that at roughly **180 million people**.

## Should you worry?

There's no evidence in the study that egoviruses make people sick. The researchers think they infect **single-celled organisms** in the gut, including some that help animals like cows and buffalo digest food, not human cells themselves.

## The smallpox connection

When the team compared egovirus genes with other viruses, egoviruses came out as the **closest known relatives of poxviruses**, the family that includes the viruses behind smallpox and mpox. Poxviruses are unusual among large DNA viruses because they infect animals directly, and their origin has been a puzzle.

The authors suggest that the shared ancestor of egoviruses and poxviruses infected tiny organisms inside animals **more than 700 million years ago**, and poxviruses later evolved to infect animal cells directly. Monash University structural biologist **Fasséli Coulibaly** told Nature that egoviruses could be the evolutionary bridge between microbe-infecting giant viruses and poxviruses. James Cook University virologist **Subir Sarker** called it "a really groundbreaking study."

That ancestry idea is still a hypothesis based on genetic comparisons. The authors also say egovirus diversity "is probably substantially undersampled, especially among wild animals."

## Why it matters

It's a reminder of how much is still unknown about the microbes we carry every day, and it shows how scientists can now discover new life just by mining public DNA data. Understanding where poxviruses came from could also help researchers understand how new ones emerge.
