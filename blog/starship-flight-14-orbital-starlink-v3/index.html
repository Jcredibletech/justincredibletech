Starship Flight 14: First Real Orbit + Starlink V3 That Actually Stays Up

SpaceX’s FCC paperwork points to a mid-September window for Starship Flight 14—the program’s first full orbital profile and the first shot at parking Starlink V3 sats in a staying orbit.

By Justin McBroom @JCredibleTech
September 11, 2026· 3 min read
𝕏

Countdown vibes are back at Starbase—and this time the paperwork says something Starship has never officially tried before: a real orbit.

SpaceX’s latest FCC filings for Flight 14 request communications authority starting September 15, 2026, and they describe an “orbital second stage.” That phrasing is the headline. Flights 1–13 flew dramatic arcs that ended the same day. Flight 14 is lined up to stay up—at least long enough to behave like a proper launch vehicle.

Fair warning before you set a calendar alert: September 15 is the start of a requested window, not a locked launch time. FAA licensing still has to clear the profile, and Starship dates love to slip. Treat it as “NET mid-September,” not “Tuesday at 3.”

What makes Flight 14 different

Two consumer-relevant milestones are stacked on this flight:

  1. First full orbital insertion profile for Starship’s upper stage (per the FCC language).
  2. ~20 Starlink V3 satellites aimed at an operational / staying orbit—the kind that can actually join the constellation.

Flight 13 already lofted a batch of production V3 hardware, but those sats were on a demo / non-service path. Flight 14 is the “okay, now leave them up there” chapter—if insertion and deployment go clean.

SpaceX has talked about V3 capacity in the neighborhood of ~1 terabit per second of downlink per satellite, with Starship eventually able to haul far more birds per flight than Falcon 9’s current Starlink rides. One successful orbital V3 drop won’t rewrite your home Wi‑Fi overnight, but it’s the bridge from “cool test rocket” to “the thing that builds the next Starlink layer.”

About those chopsticks…

The internet really wanted Flight 14 to be the first upper-stage tower catch—mech arms catching a spaceship like a video game boss fight. Early chatter floated that idea. More recent comments from Elon Musk pushed the first ship catch to “a few months” out, so the honest expectation for Flight 14 is an Indian Ocean splashdown for the ship after it finishes its work, while the Super Heavy booster keeps doing the already-familiar tower catch when conditions allow.

So the fun framing isn’t “rockets catching rockets this Tuesday”—it’s “rockets that finally stay in orbit, with catching still on the roadmap.” The chopsticks meme gets its own episode later. This episode is orbital adulthood.

Why regular people should care

Starship isn’t just a taller Falcon. It’s SpaceX’s bet that you can loft huge payloads cheaply enough to flood low Earth orbit with next-gen broadband hardware, then (eventually) go beyond LEO. For anyone who uses Starlink—or is waiting for denser capacity, lower latency, or coverage that doesn’t melt when half the neighborhood joins—orbital V3 deployments are the boring-but-huge step.

It’s also a systems test in public: guidance, thermal protection, payload doors, deorbit burns, range safety for a new trajectory class. Every successful orbit attempt turns “will it ever?” into “how often?”

What to watch for (lighthearted checklist)

When the window opens, here’s the snack-size scorecard:

  • Does the ship actually circularize / insert? (The whole point.)
  • Do the V3 sats separate cleanly? (~20 on the manifest, if reporting holds.)
  • Does the ship deorbit on purpose afterward? Ocean splashdown is success, not failure, for this profile.
  • Does the booster stick the catch? Bonus points; not the main plot this time.
  • Does the date slip again? Extremely on-brand. Bring snacks either way.

The bigger picture

Reusable boosters already made LEO launches feel routine. Starship’s job is to make heavy LEO feel routine—and Flight 14 is the first regulatory paper trail that treats the ship like an orbital workhorse instead of a suborbital stunt double.

If you’re the countdown type: keep mid-September circled in pencil. If you’re the Wi‑Fi type: root for those V3s to stay where they belong. If you’re the meme type: save the chopsticks jokes for the episode where the arms actually close.

Rockets catching rockets is coming. First, the rocket has to arrive.

Sources: SpaceX FCC STA / experimental filings describing an orbital second stage and a requested window beginning Sept 15, 2026; reporting from TeslaNorth, TechTimes, Benzinga, and Starpath summarizing Flight 14’s orbital intent, ~20 Starlink V3 payload, FAA approval still required, and Musk’s walk-back of an immediate upper-stage tower catch. Dates and manifests can change—Starship gonna Starship.
